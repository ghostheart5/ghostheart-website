I’m Not — More Than One Chapter

Get email updates

Sometimes the hardest part of telling a story comes before the first word.

You may know what you want to say and still hear all the ways it could be misunderstood. Someone could choose one sentence, one mistake, one difficult chapter, and decide they have heard enough to explain your whole life.

That fear sits at the beginning of I’m Not. Before the narrator has finished speaking, judgment is already anticipated. The wish to be understood has to share the room with the fear of being reduced to a few words.

And still, there is something worth saying.

I just want to tell my story, So maybe someone will do the same.

There is room in that word “maybe.” It leaves the outcome open. A story might reach someone. It might help them find words for something they have carried quietly. It might give them a reason to consider a next step. The narrator cannot decide what another person will take from it.

What the narrator can choose is whether to speak honestly.

That does not require a perfect ending. There can still be mistakes, uncertainty and parts that are hard to explain. A person can be learning how to live with their choices and still have something worth sharing. If every story had to wait until its teller had nothing left to work on, a great many voices would never be heard.

The song makes room for a person who kept trying.

That matters when a single chapter has become the only one anyone wants to discuss. What went wrong can take up so much space that the effort afterward becomes difficult to see: continuing, trying again, choosing what to do next. Those parts belong to the story too.

No one else can fit a whole person into the worst thing they hear about them. Listening asks more of us than that. It asks us to leave room for what we have not heard, to notice when we are filling in the gaps ourselves, and to let someone finish before we decide what their words must mean.

For the person speaking, honesty can have boundaries.

I’m just opening up the door To the parts I used to hide.

An open door does not mean every room has to be shown. You can choose the part you are ready to put into words. You can keep names, details and other people’s lives private. You can stop where you need to stop. The value of what you share does not depend on giving an audience everything.

And listening to someone else’s story places no obligation on you to reveal your own.

You can recognize a feeling without having lived the same life. You can sit with a line, think about it privately, or leave something unspoken. There is no test of how much you must disclose before the song is allowed to mean something to you.

The bridge holds on to that freedom. Hearing that someone kept going cannot undo all the hurt. The song leaves that truth intact. Its hope is smaller and more possible: perhaps there is still a next step you can choose.

Maybe that step begins with finding words. Maybe it begins with giving yourself time. It belongs to you, and no one else can decide its shape from a distance.

I’m Not ends with an invitation that leaves room for someday.

A story can be offered without applause. It can remain imperfect and still be honest. Speaking can feel frightening and still be a choice worth considering.

For anyone listening, the responsibility is simple enough to begin now: hear the person, leave room for the chapters you do not know, and resist turning one part of a life into the whole of it.

For anyone considering their own words, there is time. You can choose what belongs in the telling and what stays yours.

The fear does not have to disappear before a voice can matter.
